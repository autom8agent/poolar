# Ergonomics: will it actually play?

The design puts a ⌀24 mm collar on a cue that is ⌀14.2 mm at that station. That
is the single biggest thing you have to be comfortable with, so here is the
arithmetic rather than reassurance.

## Bridge-hand clearance

| | |
|---|---|
| Rear of bridge hand, from tip | 203–305 mm (8–12 in) |
| Collar front face | 482.6 mm |
| Clearance at address | **178 mm** |
| After a normal follow-through (50–80 mm) | ~100 mm |
| After a full break follow-through (~150 mm) | **~28 mm** |

So it clears, on every shot you would play with a 9.5 mm tip. It gets
uncomfortably close on a full-power break — and you should not be breaking with
a 9.5 mm tip anyway. **Keep a break cue.**

The shaft slides *through* your bridge; it is the forward stroke that brings the
collar toward your hand, not the backswing. If you play with an unusually long
bridge (14 in or more), redo this table with your own numbers before building
anything.

## The rest of the cue is untouched

Worth saying plainly: **nothing is bonded to the shaft.** The first 483 mm is a
clean 9.5 → 14.2 mm taper. Whatever the collar does to the look of the cue, it
does not change the part that flexes, and it does not change what your bridge
hand slides on.

## Rotational orientation

Neither joint indexes rotation, so the cue can be assembled with the lens
pointing anywhere. Two things fix that:

- the **index rib** moulded along the grip sleeve, at the lens's clock position
  — your grip hand finds it without looking
- **accelerometer-based auto-rotate** in post, because the IMU knows which way
  is down even when you don't

Do both. The rib is for playing; the auto-rotate is for the footage.

## Swing weight, not just weight

Two cues at 19 oz can feel completely different, and this one has 25 g sitting
470 mm ahead of the balance point — which raises the **moment of inertia** about
your grip hand even when the balance point and total weight are dialled in.

Practically: the cue will feel very slightly more "planted" through the stroke
than a plain cue of the same weight and balance. Some players like that. The
blank slug is how you find out — play a session with the camera in and a session
with the blank, same weight, same balance, and see whether you can tell.

That comparison is the reason the blank slug is a first-class part of this
design rather than an accessory.
